# Ethanol, Dose and Evidence: Is Alcohol Research Really Superficial?

*[Українська версія](README.uk.md)*

## The starting question

A common intuition goes like this: studying "alcohol" is like studying the effect of chamomile or St John's wort on people in general. Plants and medicines have active substances, and alcoholic drinks are mixtures too. Ethanol is the main active ingredient, but drinks also contain methanol, fusel alcohols and other compounds. Popular-science discussions, including videos and blog posts by biologist Alexander Panchin, add to the impression that the big studies and meta-analyses are one-sided or incomplete.

This essay checks that intuition against the evidence. The short answer: the criticism is partly justified, but not for the reason the chamomile analogy suggests.

## 1. Do researchers know what they are studying?

With chamomile, the problem is real: many active compounds, variable concentrations, and no single obvious thing to measure. Alcohol is different. Serious epidemiological studies measure intake in **grams of pure ethanol** per day or week, not in "drinks in general". The mechanisms are well described: ethanol is oxidised to acetaldehyde, which reacts with DNA, and the International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies alcoholic beverages as a Group 1 carcinogen.

### What about methanol and other alcohols?

Drinks do contain other alcohols and compounds (congeners): methanol, propanol, isobutanol, isoamyl alcohol, aldehydes and esters. Three facts limit their importance in legal beverages:

- **Proportions.** Ethanol is present in amounts hundreds to thousands of times greater. Wine typically contains tenths of a gram of methanol per litre against roughly 100 g of ethanol. Fruit brandies contain more methanol, vodka almost none.
- **Ethanol blocks methanol.** Both are broken down by the same enzyme, alcohol dehydrogenase, which prefers ethanol. While ethanol is in the blood, methanol is barely converted into its toxic products. That is why ethanol is used as an antidote in methanol poisoning.
- **Genetics points to ethanol.** People carrying a common ALDH2 variant clear acetaldehyde slowly, and their risk of oesophageal cancer at the same intake is much higher. If methanol or fusel alcohols were the main culprits, this gene would not matter so much.

Congeners do matter for hangover severity, and methanol in counterfeit or home-distilled spirits causes real mass poisonings. But that is a product-quality problem, not what cohort studies measure.

## 2. Where the criticism is justified

The genuine weaknesses of alcohol research are methodological:

- **Most data are observational.** Long-term randomised trials are practically impossible; a large US trial (MACH15) was cancelled in 2018 over concerns about industry funding.
- **Self-reported intake** is systematically underestimated.
- **The "sick quitter" problem.** Non-drinker groups often include people who stopped drinking because they were ill, which makes moderate drinkers look healthier. This is a major source of the famous J-shaped curve.
- **Confounding.** Moderate drinkers tend to be wealthier and more socially active.
- **Drinking pattern** (steady vs binge), genetics and food are often not accounted for.

How to correct for these biases is itself contested. Zhao, Stockwell and colleagues (2023) pooled 107 cohort studies and found no mortality benefit from low intake once former-drinker bias and study quality were adjusted for. Panchin has criticised that analysis, arguing that it effectively downgraded studies of older participants, even though any cardiovascular benefit would be expected to appear mainly with age. The counter-argument is that cohorts recruited in older age are most affected by survivor bias and sick-quitter bias. Both sides have rational grounds. The honest summary is that **the low-dose question is still open, and the effects either way are small**.

## 3. The evolutionary argument

Ethanol is natural. Fermenting fruit, nectar and honey contain it, and many animals consume it. The "drunken monkey" hypothesis proposes that our ancestors ate fermenting fallen fruit, and a mutation in the ADH4 enzyme roughly ten million years ago greatly accelerated ethanol metabolism in our lineage.

This supports the idea that very small doses are probably harmless. It does not show that typical drinking is safe:

- **Dose and concentration.** Ripe fruit usually contains well under 1% ethanol. Beer and wine appeared about 9,000 years ago, distilled spirits far more recently.
- **Adaptation to a toxin.** Enzymes that rapidly remove ethanol suggest it is something to be neutralised, not accumulated.
- **Evolution ignores old age.** Natural selection optimises survival to reproduction; cancer or cirrhosis at 60 barely affected our ancestors.
- **Natural is not the same as safe.** Sunlight and wood smoke are natural and carcinogenic.

## 4. Human evidence

| Study | Design | Main finding |
|---|---|---|
| Millwood et al., *Lancet* 2019 | 512,715 adults in China, ~10 years; Mendelian randomisation using ALDH2/ADH1B variants | Conventional analysis shows the usual U-shape, but genetically predicted intake raises blood pressure and stroke risk continuously; the apparent protection against stroke is largely non-causal. No clear effect on myocardial infarction. |
| Wood et al., *Lancet* 2018 | 599,912 current drinkers, 83 studies | Lowest all-cause mortality at about 100 g/week or less; stroke, heart failure and hypertensive disease rise roughly linearly; myocardial infarction risk falls. |
| Zhao et al., *JAMA Netw Open* 2023 | Meta-analysis, 107 cohorts, 4.8 million participants | After bias adjustment, low intake not associated with lower mortality; risk rises at higher intake. |
| Daviet et al., *Nat Commun* 2022 | MRI of 36,678 adults, UK Biobank | Little change from zero to one unit per day; reductions in grey and white matter from one to two or more units. |

### A note on genetic variation

People respond differently to alcohol, so study results are **population averages**, not individual guarantees. That does not make the studies "impure": all research on humans involves genetic diversity, large samples spread it across groups, and Mendelian randomisation studies such as Millwood's measure genetics directly.

## 5. Animal evidence (pure ethanol, standard non-modified strains)

| Organism | Dose → effect | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Nematode *C. elegans* | 0.005–0.4% ethanol extends survival of starved larvae; propanol and butanol also do, methanol does not; higher concentrations shorten life in other stages | Castro et al., *PLoS ONE* 2012 |
| *Drosophila* adults | 0.5–3% ethanol vapour gives the longest life | Parsons et al., *Aust J Zool* 1979 |
| *Drosophila* larvae | 4% in food improves survival; 12–20% kills most larvae within a day | *Sci Rep* 2021 |
| *Drosophila* adults | Low-ethanol diet increases egg production, but only with gut bacteria present | Chandler et al., *iScience* 2022 |
| Mice (C57BL/6) | 3.5% ethanol in water for life: mean lifespan +4.4%, no organ pathology | *Aging* 2020 |
| Mice (B6C3F1) | 2.5–5% ethanol for 2 years: no effect on survival, dose-related trend in liver tumours in males | NTP TR-510, 2004; Beland et al. 2005 |
| Rhesus macaques | Voluntary heavy drinking for 15 months: cortical grey matter changes | Kroenke et al., *Neuropsychopharmacology* 2014 |

The animal data show a clear dose dependence and are even less conclusive than human data about small doses.

## 6. Is ethanol special? A comparison with glucose

| Organism | Ethanol | Glucose / sugar |
|---|---|---|
| Yeast | — | 0.5% instead of 2% glucose extends lifespan by 30–40% (Lin et al., *Science* 2000) |
| Nematodes | Low doses extend survival of starved larvae | 0.1–2% glucose shortens lifespan by about 20% (Lee, Murphy & Kenyon, *Cell Metab* 2009) |
| *Drosophila* | Optimum at low concentrations, lethal at high | Optimum at intermediate sugar, excess shortens life, more in females (*Aging* 2017); effects persist after returning to a normal diet (Dobson et al., *Cell Rep* 2017); part of the harm is dehydration and disappears with added water (van Dam et al., *Cell Metab* 2020) |
| Mice | Conflicting results between strains | 25% of calories from added sugar doubles female mortality and cuts male reproduction by 25%, without obesity (Ruff et al., *Nat Commun* 2013) |

| Question | Ethanol | Glucose |
|---|---|---|
| Dose-dependent effects | Yes | Yes |
| Needed by the body | No | Yes |
| Harmful at high doses | Yes | Yes |
| Conflicting results across labs | Yes | Yes |
| Known mechanism of direct DNA damage | Yes (acetaldehyde) | No |

Ethanol behaves like many substances: "the dose makes the poison" applies even to glucose. The key differences are that glucose is essential and ethanol's metabolite directly damages DNA, so any "safe zone" for ethanol is probably narrow.

## Conclusion

- The criticism that alcohol research has weaknesses is **justified**: the data are mostly observational, self-reported and vulnerable to selection bias, and the low-dose question remains open.
- The claim that researchers study "alcohol in general" rather than the active substance is **not supported**: studies measure grams of ethanol, compare beverage types, and genetic evidence points directly to ethanol and acetaldehyde.
- "Natural, therefore safe" does **not** follow from evolution.
- Even after all corrections, only very small doses remain debatable. Harm from moderate and heavy drinking is not in serious doubt.
